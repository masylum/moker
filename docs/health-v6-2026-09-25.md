# Moker v6 health check — 25 September 2026

The economy is much less dominated by all-ins than the earlier reported 55% Riichi result, but the remaining pressure comes from tournament-end incentives, Twin Lotus and short stacks. Reset tournaments permit real recoveries. They also concentrate disproportionate influence in the last game, especially its last hand. Bots use sticks and blanks, but still do not demonstrate convincing Single Lotus bluff play. The ladder needs a targeted review of Three Winds and joker-assisted Pung, rather than a wholesale reorder.

## Scope and confidence

Completed **450 independent simulation runs, 750 constituent games and 5,963 hands**. Four players; current v6 rules and bots; **24 joint equity trials per projection**, with up to four opponent completions inside each trial; **10 CPU workers**. Simulation cohort runtimes totaled **13.1 minutes**, excluding analysis and validation. No new bot-policy or scoring changes were made during these batches.

| Cohort | Runs per mode | Format |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main | 100 | One game, four continuous orbits, 200 chips |
| Matched chip control | 25 | Same first 25 seeds, four continuous orbits, 400 chips |
| Tournament matrix | 25 at each length 1/2/3/4 | One orbit (four scheduled hands) per game; stacks reset between games |

Reset games use the **implemented** stack schedule **200 / 300 / 400 / 500**, and antes **5 / 10 / 15 / 20**. They do **not** test the previously suggested 200 / 400 / 600 / 800 schedule. Ante/starting-stack therefore rises from 2.5% to 4%; it is not proportional. Each game contributes its ending chips minus 250 per loan to cumulative tournament score. Riichi starts each game with three sticks per player. Loan allowance and elimination reset between games.

The reset matrix is a short-format test, not four orbits in every constituent game. The 1-game matrix cell is one orbit, distinct from the main four-orbit cohort. Lengths share seed prefixes, but bots know the remaining tournament length, so their paths can diverge immediately. Do not pool these cells as independent replicas of the same setting.

A “win rate” below uses fractional credit for tied winners. Opening hands are the original seven cards **before Charleston**; ordinary ladder categories partition those hands, while Lotus is analyzed separately. Showdown rows count participating hands, not all dealt hands. Improvement means a higher ordinary rank at the end, including development before a fold. These are bot-selected outcomes under the existing ladder, not intrinsic or causal card strength. Rare upper ranks remain uncertain. Approximate 95% intervals in the data are clustered by simulation run; 25-run cells have substantial uncertainty.

In four-game tournaments, last place after Game 3 supplies **20% Basic / 16% Riichi** of champion credit. My preference is to keep resets for catch-up, then separately test proportional stacks and a better final-hand policy before increasing late-game stakes further.

## Loans, Riichi declarations and sticks

In the longer main Riichi games, **19 loans in 100 games** went to 19 players. Eighteen survived through settlement, six ended with positive adjusted scores, and **two became game champions**. Loans therefore extend participation and occasionally enable a genuine comeback, while their 250-point penalty prevents a free recovery. No borrower took more than the allowed one loan per game.

Main Riichi: **303 declarations**, **284 sole-win settlements (93.7% of declarations)**, four withdrawals, **568 sticks earned**, **1,213 spent**, and **555 left** across 100 tables. High declaration success reflects selective declaration and folds as well as strong hands; it does not mean a declared hand wins 93.7% against a caller.

### Calibration judgment

1. **Review Three Winds versus Chow + Eye first.** Three Winds is higher-ranked despite being easier to receive: in 100,000 uniform deals per mode, a hand *contains* Three Winds 1.81% Basic / 1.89% Riichi, versus Chow + Eye 1.13% / 1.28%. Yet built Chow + Eye is far more common at showdown: 304 versus 94 Basic; 300 versus 126 Riichi. Initial rarity and development difficulty pull in different directions. A swap is a candidate experiment, not justified solely by initial rarity.
2. **Joker-assisted Pung merits separate scrutiny.** Riichi uniform deals contain Pung 2.09%, exceeding Three Winds at 1.89%, and 1,546 of 2,085 Pung occurrences require a Joker. Actual play turns Pung into Pung + Eye frequently: 44.0% of opening Pungs improve. This creates an especially productive route into upper ranks.
3. **Keep the rare top ranks unchanged pending targeted evidence.** The main cohort has only ten Kong, 15 Four Winds and 35 Three Dragons + Eye showdown participants. Kong requires a Joker because the deck has only three natural copies. A 9/10 result is not evidence it should rank below another hand.
4. **Treat Twin Lotus as its own balance mechanism.** Its acquisition and near-certainty matter more than where it sits on an ordinary rarity ladder.

Supporting uniform-deal results and joker dependence are in [ladder.md](health-v6-2026-09-25/ladder.md). Those 200,000 deals are cheap static evaluations, not simulated tournaments.

## Jokers, Lotus and blanks

| Showdown holdings | Participants | Fractional win rate | Ordinary rank improved by Jokers | Wins lost if own Jokers removed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| None | 1119 | 26.8% | 0 | 0 |
| Any | 537 | 42.8% | 321 | 169 |
| Green | 150 | 43.6% | 100 | 48 |
| Blue | 154 | 39.0% | 102 | 46 |
| Red | 158 | 48.3% | 110 | 63 |
| Black | 139 | 38.8% | 62 | 34 |

With any Joker: **42.8%** showdown win credit, versus **26.8%** without. Removing only that player’s Jokers from the settled showdown changes 169 wins into non-wins; summed fractional credit falls by 170. This is a **local scoring counterfactual**, not a replay of how betting/fishing would change without Jokers. Color rows overlap for multiple-Joker hands. Red has the strongest observed result; Black the weakest rank lift, but different eligible melds and selection prevent a causal color ranking.

Lotus: original deals contained **767 single-Lotus holdings and 20 Twin Lotus holdings**. There were 759 Lotus passes in Charleston, 267 normal Lotus draws, four blank claims and 715 Lotus discards. At settlement **116 player-hands retained both Lotus**, and all won: **104 uncontested, 12 at showdown; none folded**. That is 7.25% of all Riichi hands, far above the 0.326% Twin Lotus frequency in a uniform initial deal. Passing and directed acquisition amplify it substantially.

There were **zero Single Lotus bluff bonuses**. Together with eight losing Single Lotus showdown participants, this suggests bots mostly shed a single Lotus or seek its partner. The current sample does not exercise its intended bluff mechanism convincingly. Blanks are used: **366 exchanges**, including 58 during paid fishing, from 1,430 opening player-hands containing a blank. That is evidence of use, not a 25.6% conversion probability: players can acquire blanks later and exchange more than once.

## What remains behind all-ins?

First trigger per all-in hand: Basic **86 bets / 41 calls / 15 antes**; Riichi **129 / 17 / 18**. **50 of the 164 Riichi initiators held Twin Lotus**. In Basic the first initiator held High Card 44 times and Eyes 29; in Riichi, High Card 39 and Eyes 25 (including forced-ante cases). These weak-hand counts are consistent with terminal desperation, not uniformly strong value betting.

137/142 Basic and 162/164 Riichi all-ins occur before Street 4, including ante exhaustion. Early timing alone is therefore an incomplete diagnosis. Twin Lotus cannot be outdrawn under the current lock, and some calls cost a short stack very little relative to the pot. The strongest remaining concern is the nearly universal last-hand shove: a championship-oriented utility can rationally prefer variance when behind, but a crude opponent model can still choose the wrong way to create it.

## Validation and reproducibility

The ten exact replays passed. `npm run check` passed formatting, lint, dead-code analysis, TypeScript, **142 unit tests and four Worker integration tests**.
